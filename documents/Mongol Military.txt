MILITARY OF THE MONGOL EMPIRE

OVERVIEW
Starting with Genghis Khan's campaigns of 1206-1207, Mongol forces went on to conquer most of continental Asia, parts of West Asia, and parts of Eastern Europe, while also launching unsuccessful expeditions toward Japan, Java (Indonesia), and India. This military success made the Mongol Empire the largest contiguous land empire in history, second only in overall size to the British Empire.

HORSES AND MOBILITY
Each soldier generally kept three or four horses so he could switch mounts and keep moving at speed for extended periods without exhausting any single animal. Riders would dismount from a tired horse and let it travel unloaded within the herd while switching to a fresh one. Horses were also protected with lamellar armor, divided into five sections to cover the animal's body, including a special plate for the forehead tied at the neck.

ARMOR
Mongol soldiers wore lamellar armor over thick coats. This armor was built from small overlapping scales of iron, chain mail, or hardened leather, laced together with leather cord. Leather armor alone could weigh around 10 kg (22 lb), with metal-scale versions weighing more. The leather was boiled to soften it, then coated with a pitch-based lacquer for waterproofing. Some soldiers simply had metal plates sewn into a heavy coat instead of full lamellar armor.

Helmets were cone-shaped, made from iron or steel plates, and fitted with neck guards. A separate quilted cap with a large brim and earmuffs (reversible for winter use) was also worn. Whether a soldier had a metal or leather helmet depended on his rank and wealth.

ARCHERY
Mounted archers were central to Mongol armies. At the Battle of Liegnitz, a force of around 20,000 mounted archers defeated roughly 30,000 troops under Henry II of Silesia, largely through harassment and psychological pressure rather than direct assault.

The primary Mongol weapon was the composite bow, built from laminated horn, wood, and sinew - horn on the inside to resist compression, sinew on the outside to resist tension. This design let a small, horseback-friendly bow deliver serious power. Archers typically carried two or three bows (a heavier one for dismounted use, a lighter one for riding), multiple 60-arrow quivers, and files to sharpen arrowheads, which were hardened by heating and then quenching in brine.

Effective range: over 200 meters for general shooting, 150-175 meters for targeted shots (which set the standard approach distance for light cavalry), and up to 400 meters for area/ballistic shots meant to unsettle enemy troops and horses before an assault. Accuracy while shooting from a galloping horse was said to improve during the brief moment all four hooves left the ground.

The traditional Mongolian bowmaking method was suppressed under Manchu rule during the Qing dynasty and lost. What is practiced today, revived after Mongolia's 1921 independence, is based on Manchu bow designs and differs somewhat from the original Mongol Empire-era bows. Mounted archery itself only saw a broader revival in the 21st century.

CROSSBOWS AND SIEGE WEAPONS
The Mongols also used crossbows, likely adopted from the Chinese, for both infantry and cavalry, though these saw less battlefield use than the composite bow. Large numbers of siege engines and crossbows were deployed at the 1221 Siege of Nishapur against Khwarazmian forces. During the Battle of Xiangyang, both Song and Mongol forces used large siege crossbows against one another. According to the historian Juvayni, Hulagu Khan brought 3,000 large crossbows from China for the Nishapur siege, along with Chinese technicians operating a massive "ox bow" device capable of launching bolts roughly 2,500 paces at the siege of Maymun Diz.

SIEGE OF XIANGYANG AND FANCHENG (1268-1273)
These twin fortress cities blocked Mongol advance south of the Yangtze River. Song defenders received supplies by water for the first three years, until the Mongols established a full naval blockade in 1271. A relief convoy of about 100 paddle-wheel boats, led by Zhang Shun and Zhang Gui, attempted a night crossing but was detected. The Mongol fleet had spread across the entire river and stretched a chain across the water. In the resulting battle, Song forces used fire-lances, fire-bombs, and crossbows, and suffered heavy losses trying to break through the chain barrier with axes and bombs, but did reach the city walls. In 1273 the Mongols brought in a Persian and a Syrian engineer to build counterweight trebuchets, and this new siege technology led to Xiangyang's surrender that same year.

INCENDIARY AND GUNPOWDER WEAPONS
At the 1274 siege of Shaoyang, Mongol general Bayan waited for a favorable northerly wind before bombarding the city with molten-metal bombs, sparking a fire severe enough that accounts describe flames reaching "up to heaven." The city was captured and its population massacred. Bayan again used bombs at the 1275 siege of Changzhou before storming the walls, again massacring residents after they refused to surrender.

There is some evidence gunpowder weapons may have been used during the Mongol invasions of Europe - sources reference "fire catapults," "pao," and "naphtha-shooters" - though historian Timothy May notes there is no solid evidence the Mongols used gunpowder weapons regularly outside China.

Following the Mongol invasions of Japan, a Japanese scroll painting depicted a bomb (called "tetsuhau"), thought to represent the Chinese "thunder crash bomb." Japanese accounts of the invasions also describe iron and bamboo "pao" producing light, fire, and thousands of iron fragments.

TACTICS: THE KHARASH
A notable Mongol tactic involved the "kharash" - prisoners captured in earlier fighting who were driven ahead of Mongol troops during sieges and battles. These captives absorbed the brunt of enemy arrows and crossbow fire, shielding the Mongol soldiers behind them, and were also used as forced assault units to help breach walls.

INTEGRATION OF CONQUERED PEOPLES
As the Mongols conquered new territories, they incorporated the men of defeated populations into their own forces (whether those men surrendered willingly or not), which grew Mongol troop numbers as the empire expanded. The Siege of Baghdad is cited as an example where many different peoples fought together under Mongol command. Despite this integration, the Mongols generally never secured lasting loyalty from the settled populations they conquered.

COMMUNICATIONS
The Mongols built an empire-wide postal relay system of horse stations known as the "orto" (yam), enabling fast transmission of written messages - the first service of its kind on an imperial scale since the Roman Empire. On the battlefield, they communicated troop movements using signal flags, horns, and, to a lesser degree, signal arrows.
